## ABSTRACT

Email remained a primary vector for phishing, business-email-compromise (BEC) and malware
delivery, and generative language models had lowered the effort required to write polished,
convincing fraudulent messages. Rule-based and keyword filters were brittle against such
evolving, fluent attacks. This project developed an adaptive email spam detection system
that fused two complementary views of a message — its textual content and its structural
metadata — within a machine-learning pipeline, and that improved over time through
reviewed-feedback retraining.

Raw emails were parsed to extract word and character TF-IDF content features together with
twelve structural metadata signals (URL and attachment counts, sender-to-link domain
mismatch, reply-to presence, SPF/DKIM indicators, casing and urgency-term statistics). The
streams were fused into a single feature space and classified by a class-balanced logistic
regression model, with Naïve Bayes and support-vector-machine classifiers evaluated for
comparison. A FastAPI service, a browser review console, a mailbox watcher with
quarantine, and a feedback-driven retraining loop were implemented, together with an
optional deep-learning DistilBERT branch documented for GPU deployment.

The fused model was trained on 81,152 de-duplicated public emails and achieved **99.2%
accuracy**, precision 0.991, recall 0.992 and ROC-AUC 1.000 on a held-out test split, at a
median latency of approximately 16 milliseconds per email on a commodity CPU. On 4,986
phishing emails generated by GPT-4.1, DeepSeek 3.2 and LLaMA 3.3 — none of which the model
was trained on — the detector flagged **92%**, demonstrating strong generalisation to
AI-assisted phishing. After one adaptive retraining cycle on reviewed modern-threat
examples, detection ranking on held-out AI-style email improved (ROC-AUC 0.81 to 0.945)
while overall accuracy was maintained. The system detected phishing intent rather than AI
authorship, and its signals and operating threshold were fully explainable and adjustable.

## CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Problem Statement and Motivation

Electronic mail remained one of the most widely used communication channels in
organisations and one of the most exploited. Despite decades of filtering, phishing and
spam continued to cause financial loss, credential theft and malware infection. Two trends
made the problem harder. First, attacks such as business-email-compromise used calm,
professional language and often contained no malicious link at all, so they evaded
signatures designed for obvious, keyword-laden spam. Second, large language models allowed
attackers to generate fluent, personalised, typo-free fraudulent messages at scale,
removing the historical language barrier that had made many phishing emails easy to
recognise.

Conventional defences had complementary weaknesses. Pure content models examined what a
message said but ignored how it was constructed; pure rule or reputation systems used
structural indicators but were bypassed by well-written text. Static systems that were
trained once and never updated degraded as campaigns evolved. The motivation for this
project was therefore a detector that (a) fused content and metadata, (b) remained fast and
interpretable enough to run locally on ordinary hardware, and (c) adapted to new threats
through a reviewed retraining loop rather than manual rule editing.

### 1.2 Objectives

The aim was to design and implement an adaptive spam detection system that leveraged
content–metadata fusion for enhanced email security. The specific objectives, carried
forward from Project I, were:

1. To extract and process content-based features (body text, subject lines, embedded URLs)
   and metadata features (sender information, header attributes) from raw emails.
2. To design a content–metadata fusion framework that combined textual and structural data
   into a unified machine-learning pipeline.
3. To train and evaluate AI-based classification models — Naïve Bayes, logistic regression
   and support vector machines — on the fused feature set.
4. To implement an adaptive retraining mechanism that updated the model with newly reviewed
   samples, maintaining detection accuracy as spam patterns evolved.

### 1.3 Project Scope

The system was scoped to English-language email, processed and trained locally with no
dependency on external cloud APIs and no use of private employee or customer email. The
core detector was a classical machine-learning model over content and metadata; a
deep-learning transformer branch (DistilBERT) was designed and provided as an optional,
GPU-deployed enhancement. The system produced a spam probability, a binary label and an
explanation of the structural signals observed, and supported a configurable action
(report, quarantine) rather than destructive deletion. The classifier identified malicious
intent; it did not attempt to determine whether a message was authored by an AI, a task that
independent evaluations had shown to be unreliable.

### 1.4 Contributions

The project contributed: (i) a working content–metadata fusion detector with twelve
structural signals and word/character TF-IDF content features; (ii) a comparison of Naïve
Bayes, logistic regression and linear SVM classifiers on both content-only and fused
features; (iii) a complete adaptive loop — a review console, a feedback store and a
scheduled retraining path — demonstrated to improve detection on new threat types;
(iv) a deployable service layer (REST API, browser review page, mailbox watcher and
quarantine) and a documented live-mailbox integration route; and (v) an empirical
evaluation that included detection of genuine large-language-model-generated phishing.

## CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

### 2.1 Review of the Technologies

**2.1.1 Operating environment and hardware platform.** The system was developed and
evaluated on a commodity personal computer (Windows 11, Intel-class CPU, integrated Intel
UHD graphics, no discrete GPU). The design deliberately targeted CPU execution so that the
detector could run without specialist hardware; the optional deep-learning branch was
provided for free cloud-GPU execution.

**2.1.2 Firmware / operating system.** Development and testing were performed on Windows
11; all components were cross-platform Python and also executed in a Linux-based continuous
environment. No special firmware was required.

**2.1.3 Data storage.** No database server was required. Emails and labelled feedback were
stored as comma-separated-value (CSV) files, and the trained model was serialised with
Joblib. This kept the system portable and consistent with the lightweight, script-based
approach proposed in Project I.

**2.1.4 Programming language and libraries.** Python 3.13 was used. Key libraries were
scikit-learn for feature extraction and classification (TF-IDF, DictVectorizer, logistic
regression, Naïve Bayes, linear SVC), FastAPI and Uvicorn for the service, Pydantic for
validation, and Joblib for model persistence. The optional transformer branch used
Hugging Face Transformers, PyTorch and the Datasets library.

**2.1.5 Algorithms.** *TF-IDF* weighted rare and discriminative terms; word unigrams/bigrams
captured phrasing while character n-grams (3–5) tolerated obfuscation such as spaced or
misspelled words. *Logistic regression* provided a calibrated-style probability and
interpretable coefficients; *multinomial Naïve Bayes* offered a fast generative baseline;
*linear support vector machines* provided a strong discriminative baseline. *Standardisation*
was applied to metadata so that raw lengths did not dominate the normalised text features.
Deep-learning transformers (DistilBERT) modelled context and paraphrase at higher cost.

**2.1.6 Summary of the technology review.** A Python/scikit-learn stack gave a fast,
explainable, reproducible detector on commodity hardware, while the documented transformer
path provided a route to semantic deep learning when a GPU was available.

### 2.2 Review of Existing Systems and Approaches

**2.2.1 Content-based detection.** Statistical content filters (e.g. Naïve Bayes over bag of
words) had been effective for classic spam but were vulnerable to fluent, reworded messages
and ignored message structure.

**2.2.2 Metadata-based detection.** Header and structural analysis (sender domain, SPF/DKIM,
reply-to, links) caught structural anomalies but was evaded by well-formed, link-less BEC.

**2.2.3 Hybrid / fusion approaches.** Combining content and metadata was reported to be more
robust than either alone; this project adopted and extended that principle with an explicit
fusion pipeline and an adaptive retraining loop.

**2.2.4 Deep-learning-based detection.** BERT-family models achieved strong accuracy on
public corpora (published work reported roughly 99% across Enron, SpamAssassin, CEAS and
related sets) but required greater compute and were less directly explainable.

**2.2.5 AI-generated phishing.** Recent research (Phish-Master, 2025) showed that
LLM-generated phishing could evade an existing institutional filter at very high rates,
while a machine-learning detector trained on an ordinary phishing dataset detected
approximately 99.9% of those LLM-generated emails — evidence that conventional models can
generalise to AI-assisted phishing. Public LLM-phishing corpora (GPT/DeepSeek/LLaMA
generated) and the BEC-2 business-email-compromise dataset became available and were used in
this study.

**2.2.6 Commercial systems and limitations.** Commercial filters such as Gmail used
proprietary, continuously updated AI pipelines and reported blocking over 99.9% of unwanted
mail; because their models were private, they could not be benchmarked offline, and any
comparison must be a controlled, authorised black-box test. Table 2.1 summarises the
paradigms.

**Table 2.1 — Comparison of detection paradigms**

| Approach | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Rules / keywords | Simple, explainable | Brittle, easily evaded |
| Content (TF-IDF/NB) | Fast, learns wording | Misses structure/fluent rewording |
| Metadata / reputation | Catches structural anomalies | Evaded by link-less BEC |
| Hybrid fusion | Robust, balanced | More features to manage |
| Deep learning (BERT) | Context/paraphrase aware | Costly, less explainable |
| This project (fusion + adaptation) | Robust, fast, explainable, updates from feedback | Semantic edge cases; optional GPU branch |

### 2.3 Limitations of Previous Studies

Many studies reported scores on random splits of single corpora where class sources were
stylistically distinct, overstating real-world generalisation; few fused rich metadata with
content in an explainable, locally deployable form; and few demonstrated adaptation to new
threat types or to LLM-generated phishing with honest framing.

### 2.4 Proposed Solution

The proposed solution was a local, open-source adaptive detector fusing word/character
TF-IDF with twelve metadata signals under class-balanced logistic regression, compared
against Naïve Bayes and SVM, wrapped in a service with a review/feedback loop and an
optional transformer branch.

## CHAPTER 3: SYSTEM METHODOLOGY / APPROACH

### 3.1 System Architecture Diagram

The pipeline proceeded from incoming email to action and feedback:

```
            incoming email (.eml / API JSON / mailbox watcher)
                              |
              +---------------+----------------+
              |        feature extraction       |
              |  content: word + char TF-IDF     |
              |  metadata: 12 structural signals |
              +---------------+----------------+
                              |
            FeatureUnion fusion -> standardisation
                              |
            class-balanced Logistic Regression
                              |
                spam probability + fired signals
                              |
        label / quarantine  ---->  review queue / console
                              |
        reviewed corrections -> feedback dataset
                              |
        scheduled retrain (adaptation) -> redeploy
```

### 3.2 Use Cases

The primary actor was an IT administrator / reviewer. Key use cases were: submit an email
for scoring; receive a label, probability and signal explanation; review and quarantine
flagged mail; correct misclassifications (feedback); trigger or schedule retraining; and
serve predictions to a mail automation workflow via the API.

### 3.3 Activity / Process Flow

Emails were parsed and feature-extracted; the model produced a probability compared against a
configurable threshold; high-risk mail was quarantined or labelled; uncertain or corrected
cases entered the review queue; on a schedule, reviewed labels were appended to the training
set and a new model was fitted, validated, and redeployed.

### 3.4 Datasets

The training corpus was the public Kaggle "Phishing Email Dataset" (CC BY-SA 4.0), merging
six established corpora — Enron, Ling-Spam and SpamAssassin (mostly legitimate) and
CEAS-2008, Nazario and Nigerian-Fraud (phishing/fraud). After conversion and de-duplication,
**81,152 unique emails** remained (39,234 legitimate; 41,918 phishing), split 75% train /
25% held-out test with stratification. A separate, disjoint modern-threat test set and a
reviewed feedback set were constructed synthetically for adaptation experiments, and the
4,986-email cross-model LLM-phishing corpus was used solely as an external test.

### 3.5 Feature Extraction

*Content features* were subject/body TF-IDF over word unigrams/bigrams (up to 40,000 terms)
and character n-grams of length 3–5 (up to 30,000 features). *Metadata features* were twelve
signals: subject length, body length, URL count, unique link domains, attachment count,
sender-has-domain flag, sender-domain/link-domain mismatch, reply-to presence, SPF-pass and
DKIM-present indicators, subject all-caps ratio, exclamation count, and a
suspicious/urgency-term count. Metadata was standardised (StandardScaler, sparse-safe) so
that raw lengths could not dominate the normalised text features.

### 3.6 Fusion and Classification

A scikit-learn FeatureUnion concatenated the three streams (word, character, metadata)
into one vector. Three classifiers were evaluated: multinomial Naïve Bayes, class-balanced
logistic regression, and a linear support vector machine. Logistic regression was selected
for deployment because it produced a probability, supported balanced class weighting and
gave interpretable signal contributions.

### 3.7 Evaluation Design and Performance Definitions

Performance was measured with accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and ROC-AUC, together with
the confusion matrix and the false-positive rate, because a legitimate message wrongly
quarantined was the most costly error. A threshold sweep reported recall at a low (≤5%)
false-positive operating point. Per-message latency (p50/p95) was measured on CPU.
External tests assessed generalisation to modern/AI-style and LLM-generated phishing, and
an before/after adaptation experiment measured improvement from reviewed retraining.
Validity controls were: strict train/test separation, no training on test emails, and use
of inert placeholder domains in synthetic data.

## CHAPTER 4: SYSTEM DESIGN

### 4.1 System Block Diagram

The system comprised five blocks: (1) ingestion (API endpoint, mailbox watcher, or file),
(2) parsing and feature extraction, (3) the fusion classifier, (4) action/decision
(labelling, quarantine, signal explanation), and (5) the adaptation block (review queue,
feedback store, scheduled retraining). Each block was implemented as an independent,
testable Python module.

### 4.2 Component Specifications

- `features.py` — robust RFC-5322 parsing; content and metadata extraction; crash-safe URL
  handling and a fallback for malformed mail.
- `model.py` — the `EmailSpamDetector` class: fusion pipeline, fit/predict, save/load, and a
  `Prediction` result carrying label, probability, confidence and signals.
- `api.py` — FastAPI service exposing `/health`, `/predict`, a `/feedback` endpoint and a
  browser paste-and-check page with review buttons.
- `scan_mailbox.py` — folder scanning with report/quarantine/watch modes, logging each
  decision to a review queue.
- `feedback.py` — converts reviewer corrections in the queue into a labelled feedback CSV.
- `prepare_dataset.py` — ingests and merges public corpus CSVs, de-duplicates, and handles
  encodings, long fields and multiple label formats.
- `evaluate.py` / `evaluate_external.py` — held-out metrics, threshold sweeps and
  recall-at-low-FPR reporting.
- `transformer.py` / `train_transformer.py` / `notebooks/distilbert_colab.ipynb` — optional
  DistilBERT branch and free-GPU training notebook.

### 4.3 Component Interaction

Ingestion passed raw email text to the feature block, which returned a record of content and
metadata. The classifier returned a probability; the action block compared it to the
threshold and emitted a label and signals, optionally moving the file to quarantine. The
review block wrote each decision with a blank correction field; reviewer corrections flowed
through `feedback.py` into a feedback CSV consumed by the next scheduled retraining run,
whose output model replaced the deployed artefact.

### 4.4 Reproducibility

All randomness used fixed seeds; the model artefact was serialised with Joblib; the data
conversion, training and evaluation steps were one-line commands so that the entire system
could be rebuilt from the public corpus.

## CHAPTER 5: SYSTEM IMPLEMENTATION

### 5.1 Hardware and Software Setup

The system ran on a Windows 11 laptop with a CPU-only Python 3.13 virtual environment.
Dependencies were installed from `requirements.txt` (scikit-learn, Joblib, FastAPI,
Uvicorn, Pydantic); the optional transformer stack was isolated in
`requirements-transformer.txt` and executed on a cloud GPU notebook.

### 5.2 Settings and Configuration

The deployed decision threshold was set to 0.55 by default and re-tuned per deployment using
the threshold sweep. The fusion model used class-balanced logistic regression with
regularisation `C=1.5`, word TF-IDF (1–2 grams, 40k features), character TF-IDF (3–5 grams,
30k features) and standardised metadata.

### 5.4 Implementation Issues and Challenges

Several real-world issues were encountered and resolved. (a) Public-corpus CSV fields
exceeded Python's default CSV field limit, which was raised on package import. (b) Malformed
bracketed/IPv6-like URLs crashed the URL parser, so URL extraction was made crash-safe with a
fallback. (c) Unscaled metadata (e.g. raw body length) dominated the normalised text
features until metadata standardisation was added, after which short phishing messages were
classified correctly. (d) One source corpus file was a redundant pre-merged copy and was
de-duplicated automatically. (e) A Windows application-control policy initially blocked a
scientific-library binary; this was resolved by installing official prebuilt packages.

### 5.5 Concluding Remark

The implementation produced a complete, local, end-to-end detector with a service, a
review/adaptation loop and documented integrations, satisfying the four project objectives.

**6.2.3 Detection of genuine LLM-generated phishing.** Tested on 4,986 phishing emails
generated by GPT-4.1, DeepSeek 3.2 and LLaMA 3.3 (external set, never trained on), the
baseline detector caught **92% (4,565/4,986)** at a low-threshold operating point and 84% at
the default 0.55 threshold, at roughly 11 ms per email. The catch rate fell as the threshold
rose, indicating the model was systematically less confident on LLM-written mail than on
classic phishing — evidence that AI-assisted messages were harder and a motivation for
adaptation and the transformer branch.

**6.2.4 Adaptive improvement (Objective 4).** After retraining on a disjoint reviewed batch
of modern AI-style/BEC emails, detection ranking on the held-out modern set improved from
ROC-AUC 0.81 to **0.945**, and phishing recall at a near-zero false-positive point rose from
20% to **85%**, while main-corpus accuracy was unchanged (0.991). A self-contained
demonstration (`demo_adaptive.py`) showed a new campaign going from 0/6 to 5/6 caught after
one reviewed retraining cycle. The improvement was greatest for threat types actually
represented in the reviewed feedback, which was reported honestly.

### 6.3 Discussion and Project Challenges

The fused model matched published results on the same public corpora, and generalised
strongly to AI-generated phishing without any AI-specific training, supporting the
content–metadata fusion approach. False positives were the dominant practical concern and
motivated both the metadata/standardisation work and the configurable threshold. The
deep-learning branch was expected to reduce residual semantic false positives but required a
GPU. Threats to validity were stated explicitly: the 99.2% figure came from a held-out split
of a combined public corpus in which class sources were stylistically distinct, so
real-organisation performance would need separate validation; body-only external emails could
not exercise header metadata; and the system detected intent, not AI authorship.

## CHAPTER 7: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

### 7.1 Conclusion

An adaptive email spam detection system using AI and content–metadata fusion was designed,
implemented and evaluated. It fused word/character content features with twelve structural
metadata signals in a class-balanced machine-learning model, achieving 99.2% accuracy on a
large public benchmark at roughly 16 ms per email on a CPU. It flagged 92% of previously
unseen, large-language-model-generated phishing, and measurably improved on new threat types
after a single reviewed retraining cycle while preserving accuracy. The system was deployed
as a local service with a review console, mailbox quarantine and feedback-driven adaptation,
demonstrating that a fast, transparent fusion model with a sound adaptation loop provides a
practical, defensible defence against modern, AI-assisted email threats.

### 7.2 Recommendations / Future Work

1. Fine-tune the optional DistilBERT branch on a GPU (a ready-to-run Google Colab notebook was
   provided) and fuse its semantic score with the metadata-aware baseline to reduce residual
   semantic false positives.
2. Integrate the API with a live mailbox through an automation platform or mail-transfer agent,
   with quarantine and a reviewer dashboard.
3. Perform a supervisor-approved, controlled black-box comparison against a commercial filter
   on a disposable account, reporting results on the shared test set only.
4. Operationalise periodic scheduled retraining with drift monitoring, retraining on reviewed
   genuine new-campaign samples.
5. Evaluate on additional organisational and multilingual mail after appropriate approvals.
